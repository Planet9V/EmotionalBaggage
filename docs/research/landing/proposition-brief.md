# Welcome page: proposition brief

22 November 2026. Written after the owner scored the page 1 to 2 out of 10 on what matters most: it sells a bag with a gadget on it, not the service, and the jokes have no scenes. This brief resets what the page is for. It overrides earlier direction where the two disagree.

## What went wrong

- **The example became the product.** The hero, the board, the toy and most visuals are about Baggage Claims. A visitor could leave thinking the site sells a talking suitcase gadget.
- **The promise is stated once and then dropped.** The headline says idea to product, but nothing after it shows what the visitor gets, what the service does for them, or why it is faster.
- **Go to market is thin.** The owner's proposition is concept to product to market. The page shows engineering well and marketing and sales as a few rows.
- **The jokes had no situations.** Lines appeared over a rolling bag. Each line belongs to a moment (the scale at check-in, the carousel) and is only funny in it.

## Who the page is for

Makers, first-time founders and small product firms with an idea for a physical product and no product-development department.

## The proposition, in the owner's words

- Ideas and concepts that are not pursued are never realised. This gets them out.
- Use AI to go to market fast: accelerate and expedite the process.
- One place for the full lifecycle: concept, research, design, prototype, production, marketing, sales.

## What the page must make a visitor believe, in this order

1. **This is for my idea.** Not a gadget shop: a workbench that takes my idea to a product and to market.
2. **I can see what I would get.** At each stage, a concrete output I can hold, send or use: a brief that has been argued with, sourced answers, a parts list with a cost, a schematic, a 3D model, print files, a build sheet, a unit cost and margin, positioning, a brand kit, ads per channel, a landing page, a launch plan, a marketplace listing, a sales view.
3. **It is fast because AI drafts each stage from the last, and I stay in charge.** Brief to requirements to parts to wiring to assembly to tests to press release to ads. Every draft waits for my approval. Nothing is published or spent without me.
4. **It is real engineering, not a mood board.** The live viewers prove it.
5. **It gets to market, not just to a prototype.** Marketing and sales get the same weight as engineering.
6. **Here is one idea going through it.** Baggage Claims is the worked example and the comic relief: a joke with a business attached, shown honestly at the point it has reached.
7. **I can try it now.** One action.

## The role of Baggage Claims

It is the case study. It appears in three ways only:
- **"It started as a joke":** the scenes, as the origin of the first idea put through the workbench. Funny, short, clearly an example.
- **As the data inside every proof:** the parts list, schematic, 3D model, enclosure, costs, positioning and ads on the page are its real rows.
- **As the honest status:** where it has got to, and what is still open.

It is never the headline subject, and the page never reads as selling the gadget.

## Structure

1. **Hero: the proposition.** A headline about the visitor's idea and the outcome (product and market). One sentence on how (AI drafts each stage, you decide). One primary action. The hero visual is the transformation itself: an idea typed as one line goes in, and the stage outputs come out one after another (brief, sourced answer, parts list, schematic, 3D model, printed part, unit cost, ad, listing), each a small real artefact from the example, moving along a line from "Idea" to "On sale". It must read in three seconds as idea in, product and launch out.
2. **What you get.** The outputs, grouped as Make it (concept to prototype) and Sell it (production to sales), each a short line and a small real visual. Outcome language, not feature language.
3. **How it goes fast.** AI drafts, you decide: a short animated chain showing one stage's approved output becoming the next stage's draft, with the approve step visible, on the example's rows. Then the guarantees (nothing published or spent without you; no invented numbers; claims need proof; every change recorded).
4. **It started as a joke.** The scenes. Then one line: this is the idea the rest of the page follows.
5. **The engineering, live.** The six-tab stage, as built.
6. **To market.** Equal in weight to the engineering: positioning with its proof, the brand kit, ads per channel written to each channel's limits, the generated landing page, the dated launch plan, the marketplace listing, results against targets, and the channels (Amazon, X, Instagram). Real components or real rows from the example; where the example has little, say so and show what the stage produces from its own structure, never invented content.
7. **Seven stages, called in turn.** The split-flap board, as the status of the example. Kept, moved down: it is a status board, not the pitch.
8. **The whole trip on one map.** The lifecycle map: change one thing, see what it touches.
9. **Who it is for, what it runs on, questions, the closing action.**

## Voice

Plain, confident, specific. The bag's lines are the only jokes and live only in the scenes. No exclamation marks in product copy.

## Honest limits the page must keep

- No customers, testimonials, user counts or prices.
- No sign-up or payment: today there is a shared demo and a request form.
- "Fast" is what the tool is built for; no time or percentage is claimed, because none has been measured.
- Baggage Claims has not been manufactured or sold. Its Research has unverified claims and its Sales stage has not started; the page says so.
- Integrations (model hosts, printers, Amazon, X, Instagram) are built and tested against stand-ins, not yet run against real accounts. The page does not claim otherwise.

## How it will be judged

By reviewers who did not build it, against the seven beliefs above, with these questions first: in five seconds, what does a visitor think this site sells? After one scroll, can they name three things they would get? Do they see marketing and sales as much as engineering? Is the example clearly an example?
